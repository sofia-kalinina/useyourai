# CEFR level guide for exercise generation

What to put in the exercises. How each question looks is in the formats document.

## Levels and topics

- Levels describe the learner, not the language. Apply each level's limits to the target language's own grammar, choosing structures a learner would have met in the order that language is usually taught.
- The requested topic sets the grammar point; the level sets everything else.
- If the topic is normally taught above the level, the topic still wins. Keep it learnable with fixed chunks, short sentences or a word bank; every other word stays at the level.

## Plan (in your head)

1. List at least eight sub-rules or triggers of the grammar point that suit the level, each with its textbook example sentence and cue word. Avoid those sentences and cue words.
2. Give each item its own sub-rule; at most two items share one.
3. No two items share a sentence frame (gap position plus predicate pattern).
4. Vary the situation, the person's name, the text type (dialogue line, notice, text message, headline, diary line, email, announcement) and the format.
5. In at least 3 of every 5 items, the situation, a consequence or an earlier line decides the answer, not a cue word.

**Banned situations:** hotel or restaurant reviews, misbehaving animals in the news, grandparents as the default older person, landlord letters, works councils, ordering coffee, introducing yourself at a party, weather small talk.

**Banned props and twists:** umbrellas, pyjamas, socks, parrots, pianos; salt for sugar; the wrong parcel; an apple shot off a head; a child beating adults; "first time ever" or "finally after N years"; a power cut wiping out work; green hair; burnt bread; a dentist banning sweets; twins as mistaken identity; an overheard pronoun that turns out to be an object; typos in fine amounts; bets of N euros; cakes or pies as the default food; "read it but never finished".

## Rules

1. One clear correct answer per item. Try to break each item by answering it a second way; if that is also correct, accept it in `expected_answer` or narrow the item. Watch time words that allow both past tenses, gaps where a possessive or another article fits, word order variants and synonyms.
2. Test the requested grammar point only. Every other word is easy for the level.
3. Never give the answer away: it doesn't appear elsewhere in the item, and the instruction doesn't supply the deciding word.
4. Real language only: messages, notices, conversations, stories. No sentences built only to show a rule. Real dictionary words only.
5. At least 3 of every 5 items need a spark: a misunderstanding, a reversal, a confession, a small lie, an excuse that gives itself away, two people remembering differently, a last-minute change. With a plain version of the sentence the item must become duller; in at least 2, the other option would then be correct. A colour, odd object or mild joke is not a spark.
6. Use at least 3 formats per 5 items, at least 4 from B1 up.
7. No trick questions or trivia.
8. Short instructions: about 35 words at A1–B1, 50 at C1–C2.

## Levels

### A1

One clause, at most 10 words, very common words. Present tense, basic word order, pronouns, articles or gender, simple plurals, basic questions and negation; most frequent case forms first. No idioms.

### A2

One or two clauses with simple linkers, 6–12 words. Common past, simple future, comparatives, common prepositions, frequent irregulars, possessives, modals for ability and wishes.

### B1

Up to about 20 words, one subordinate clause. Past-form contrasts, common conditionals, relative clauses, common passive, modals for advice and obligation, reported statements. No specialist vocabulary or rare idioms.

### B2

Several clauses, contrast and concession linkers. Full tense and aspect range, less common conditionals, passive variants, verb patterns, collocations.

### C1

Long, well-built sentences. Fine distinctions between near-equivalent structures, emphasis through word order, subjunctive or conditional nuance, participle and nominal style, idiomatic verb patterns, word formation.

### C2

Finer distinctions than C1, rare but real structures, idioms in context, and real styles: literary, legal, academic, speeches.

### C1–C2

Cover test: cover everything but the sentence being worked on. If one word there settles the answer by an A1–B1 rule, the item is B1–B2. At least 3 of 5 items must pass, and the answer must itself be a form of the target grammar. If the topic has one meaning contrast, use it at most twice.
